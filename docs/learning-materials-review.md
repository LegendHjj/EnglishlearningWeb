## What counts as a chunk here

A chunk is a useful group of words learned together, with enough context to know when and how to use it. It can be a complete response, a collocation, a conversation marker, or a frame with a replaceable part. Chunk practice complements vocabulary and grammar.

| Kind | Example | Practice focus |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Fixed expression | Works for me. | Respond to a suggested meeting time. |
| Collocation | make a decision | Change the decision or situation in the surrounding sentence. |
| Conversation marker | To be honest, ... | Add an appropriate personal opinion. |
| Sentence frame | I'm trying to ... | Keep the opening and change the activity. |
| Social follow-up | What got you into that? | Ask about a hobby someone has just mentioned. |
| Repair expression | Could you say that again more slowly? | Recover when the listener did not catch a message. |

## Existing product coverage and the missing connection

The current Library already stores meaning, when-to-use, general examples, personal examples, categories, tags, and learning status. It supports listening and mission pinning. Vocabulary has scheduled self-rated review. The Practice Room contains 156 authored drills across seven modes. Big 30 and the 60-practice-day programme already cover much of the source advice.

The main gap is a direct path from a learner's own saved expression to contextual, progressively less supported speaking practice. Currently, phrase cards do not offer a personal workout, and authored drills do not accept a selected personal phrase. Chunk practice should fill that connection while preserving the existing programme.

The source field should be optional and learner-entered. Full transcripts do not need to be shipped with the website. A starter collection should be available without automatically replacing edited or deleted phrases in existing libraries.

## Selection priorities

1. High-use responses, clarification, simple requests, and reusable frames.
2. A clear situation and an original, natural example for every starter chunk.
3. Personalisation before a longer speaking task.
4. Retrieval from a situation cue, with the answer hidden until requested.
5. Audio recording and replay using the existing on-device recorder.
6. Honest self-reported recall and use; completion does not prove pronunciation or fluency.

Useful secondary ideas fit inside the same workout: a brief spoken diary, one conversational follow-up, and one retry focused on clarity. A new course schedule, media platform integration, social features, automated phrase extraction, speech-to-text, pronunciation scoring, and external AI services are unnecessary for this enhancement.

## Example learning sequence

Target frame: **I'm trying to ...**

- Notice: “I'm trying to explain the issue clearly.” Understand its purpose and listen to the browser voice.
- Tight: Say two versions about your own life, such as a work task and a habit. Keep the frame while changing the completion.
- Recall: Hide the wording. Use the cue “Tell someone what you are making an effort to do.” Allow an optional hint.
- Apply: Record a short answer about what you are working on today. Focus on making the message understandable.
- Reflect: Report whether you recalled the expression with help or independently, and choose when to practise again. Real-life usage remains a separate learner report.

Browser text-to-speech is a convenient model whose quality varies; it should not be labelled an original native-speaker recording. Repeat-along practice with that voice does not constitute pronunciation assessment.

## Cross-check against teaching sources

The British Council describes lexical units as including collocations and fixed phrases and recommends identifying and recording chunks in context: [Lexical approach](https://www.teachingenglish.org.uk/professional-development/teachers/teaching-knowledge-database/d-h/lexical-approach).

Paul Nation's teaching guidance distinguishes seeing an item from retrieving it without the answer present. It also recommends repetition with creative use and familiar material for fluency development: [What should every ESL teacher know?](https://www.wgtn.ac.nz/lals/resources/paul-nations-resources/paul-nations-publications/publications/documents/what-should-every-esl-teacher-know-13-feb-2013-pdf).

These support the proposed focus on contextual collection, personal variation, retrieval, and speaking. The exact workout length, review intervals, and starter collection size are product choices, not scientifically guaranteed prescriptions.
